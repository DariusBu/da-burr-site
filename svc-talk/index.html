How to stabilize recurrent excitation

The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron

Every neuron in the talk is this: a leaky integrator of its input current, a threshold, a reset, and quantal release at its synapses. Purple square = the neuron, it flashes when it spikes; green = synaptic currents.

The external input sets the leak potential (orange). Below threshold the neuron fires only on the membrane noise (grey strip): the rate against the input is the grey curve, and the live point sits on it.

Every square is one neuron (or one recorded unit), lit when it spikes and fading over 100 ms. Dark frames are recordings, 81 units each: mouse V1 and hippocampus from an Allen Neuropixels session (awake, no stimulus), macaque motor cortex from two Utah-array sessions of a reach-to-grasp task. Dashed frames are the model, running live, coloured by regime: blue noise driven, green reverberating, red the states that are avoided (silent is drawn, not simulated; runaway is slowed 50-fold, each neuron fires at 400 Hz).

Left to right: how much the neurons fire together (mean pairwise correlation of 100 ms counts). Bottom to top: how irregular each neuron fires (CV of the interspike interval, 1 = Poisson). All four recordings are irregular (CV 1.15 to 1.65) and weakly correlated (0.01 to 0.16). The model’s noise-driven (the sweep’s “input driven”) and reverberating regimes are more regular (CV 0.5 to 0.6) at similar correlations. Bursting is at the right: the population fires in volleys (correlation 0.75, CV 1.1).

The bottom band and the right band are where biology does not go: clock-like regular firing (no recurrence, input above threshold, CV 0.1), a silent network, runaway excitation (static synapses: every neuron at its refractory limit, 400 Hz, CV 0.02), and the fully synchronous bursts. The next slides ask what keeps a recurrent network out of them.

Recurrent excitation requires a negative feedback

One network. The slider is the external input; the top (-50 mV) puts the resting potential at threshold. Click a square to make one neuron spike.

Start with no recurrence (grey): the rate is the single neuron’s response to the input. Switch the recurrent excitation on (+, purple): a single spike is enough to set off the loop; every neuron ends up firing at its refractory limit, whatever the input. Switch the negative feedback on (−, blue): the rate follows the input again.

Why it blows up, and how to prevent it

The same microcircuit three times: 60 presynaptic units firing at the in-rate converge on one neuron (the network’s parameters). Spikes travel along the axons, released quanta (green) jump into the neuron, the neuron’s spikes leave to the right. Below: in rate, the mean synaptic current, out rate.

Left, static synapses: the current grows with the in-rate and the out-rate overshoots it: in a recurrent network this is the runaway. Middle: a fifth of the inputs are inhibitory (blue) and fire at the same rate, so their current cancels the excitatory one on average: the out-rate follows the input only. Right: depleting synapses: each spike releases less the faster the unit fires, so the current saturates. Both change the response function from in-rate to current.

Response function with synaptic depression

One synapse. Each spike (from the left) releases every filled slot with probability 0.2; each empty slot refills at the recovery rate k (default 1/12 s), the vesicle fading in. Released vesicles leave to the right. Under each train, its rate: the events currently on the lane, divided by the 2 s they take to cross.

Raise the presynaptic rate: the slots empty out, each spike releases less, and the vesicle rate stops growing at the dashed line N · k: at steady state release equals refill, and refill (empty slots × k) can never exceed N · k. Raise k: the plateau moves up. The cap is a property of the synapse, not of the input.

Network effect of the negative feedback

Two networks, same external input (the slider on top). Top: 80 excitatory and 20 inhibitory neurons (blue), static synapses, inhibitory synapses 4 times stronger. Bottom: purely excitatory with depletion; each neuron’s square shrinks as its primed pool empties.

Middle: the inhibitory population tracks the excitatory one (the diagonal: balance), and the depletion network’s missing resources rise with its rate (the negative feedback). Right: the mean recurrent synaptic current over time. Both are insensitive to the input, at different values: ~0 with inhibition (excitation and inhibition cancel: subtractive), a flat ~0.5 mV with depletion (the release rate saturates: a multiplicative cap).

The full vesicle cycle

The full cycle, one vesicle at a time. A spike releases each primed vesicle with probability 0.2; the released vesicle (empty, dashed) returns to the fused membrane. From there, vesicles are recovered at a fixed trickle of one per 3 s (solid outline), loaded (filled again), docked, and primed. The trickle is the bottleneck: it is what caps the vesicle rate, whatever the spike rate.
